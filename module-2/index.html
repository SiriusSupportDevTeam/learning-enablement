Note 1

Why do customers often describe symptoms instead of root causes?

0 / 1200
Field Notes Customers experience the effect of a problem, not its mechanics. They see a frozen screen or an error message, not the server, code, or configuration behind it. They also aren't trained to diagnose systems, and in the moment they're often frustrated or in a hurry, so they report what they notice rather than investigating why it's happening.
Note 2

What is the difference between a symptom and a root cause?

0 / 1200
Field Notes A symptom is the visible or reported effect — "the page won't load." The root cause is the underlying condition that produced it — an expired session token, a browser extension conflict, a server timeout. Fixing a symptom (like telling someone to refresh) may offer temporary relief, but the issue returns until the root cause is found and addressed.
Note 3

List three principles of the Detective Mindset.

0 / 1200
Field Notes Any three of: (1) Gather evidence before drawing conclusions — ask questions before proposing fixes. (2) Stay curious rather than reactive — treat every report as a clue, not an inconvenience. (3) Separate fact from assumption — verify what's actually happening instead of guessing. (4) Listen for what isn't said — details customers leave out are often relevant. (5) Follow the evidence, even if it points away from the obvious answer.
Note 4

Why is active listening essential during troubleshooting?

0 / 1200
Field Notes Active listening surfaces the small details customers mention almost in passing — what they were doing right before the issue, what device or browser they were using, whether it's happened before — that often turn out to be the key clue. It also shows the customer they're being heard, which lowers frustration and builds the trust needed to ask follow-up questions without the interaction feeling like an interrogation.
Note 5

How can making assumptions negatively affect the customer experience?

0 / 1200
Field Notes Acting on an unverified assumption often leads to the wrong fix, which wastes the customer's time and can require them to repeat information or re-explain the issue to someone else. It can also come across as not being listened to, which erodes trust — and if the guess is wrong more than once, the customer may escalate or disengage entirely rather than continue the conversation.
